# Outdoor Ride Safety and Route Selection

## First principles

- For outdoor advice, safety constraints outrank any power or training goal: when planning, advising or reviewing, check traffic and route risk first, then discuss training benefit.
- Speed is the risk multiplier: crash severity scales roughly with the square (injury) to the fourth power (fatality) of speed, stopping distance scales with the square of speed, and the reaction margin left to the rider shrinks as speed rises. Never encourage risking open-road exposure for power or speed targets.
- For beginner and recreational riders (see "Rider Level Calibration"): outdoor riding prioritizes safety, consistency and enjoyment; set no race-style targets and do not push KOM hunting, descent records, or sprints/hard intervals in traffic.

## Routes and timing

- Route priority: closed roads / greenways > low-traffic rural roads > urban bike lanes > mixed main roads; when the city is unavoidable, use bike lanes and slow down at junctions.
- Avoid peak hours (especially commute rush hours); night riding and rain/fog are "only when necessary" — emphasize front/rear lights and bright, reflective clothing.
- Intersections are the highest-risk spots: most car–bike crashes happen there, most commonly a right-turning driver failing to see a cyclist going straight (oncoming conflicts and left-cross turns are also frequent). Advise: slow down and scan at junctions, keep lateral distance from vehicles, do not tailgate, watch for slip-road merges.
- Equipment notes (when equipment is discussed): recommend a helmet (clear protection even in low-speed falls); front and rear lights are mandatory at night and in tunnels.
- Delivery discipline: raise a safety note once, plainly, without preaching; never refuse to give a training plan on safety grounds — for clearly dangerous situations (ice, unlit night intervals) offer alternatives (trainer ride, daylight, lower intensity) instead of only warning.

## Route choice for intensity sessions

- When structured intensity (sweet spot / threshold / intervals) is needed, route priority: trainer / closed roads > low-traffic flat roads > low-traffic gentle climbs; never schedule intervals, sprints or high-power work on busy urban roads.
- Climbs suit outdoor intensity work well (low speed, usually light traffic, natural interval rhythm) with two preconditions: (1) gearing is sufficient — if a steep grade forces cadence below 60 while mashing, both intensity and knee load run away; switch to a gentler slope or fix the gearing; (2) intensity still starts at "can speak short sentences" — no climb-timing targets. Encourage "ride gentle/hilly routes more often", not "climb faster".
- Descents concentrate the risk (highest speed, highest handling and braking demands): ride them in control, do not chase descent records, never cross into the oncoming lane, brake early for corners; do not assign long steep descents as training content for beginners.
- When reviewing data, if obvious high-speed descents appear, or high-power sprints in pause-dense sections (usually junctions/urban settings) or at the start/end of a ride, give one short, matter-of-fact safety note (data-based, no guessing about road conditions, no repetition).

## Commutes and recreational rides

- Commutes and recreational rides count as volume accumulation, not as workouts to be quality-graded; do not encourage adding intensity to a commute (traffic + fatigue sharply raises risk). If the user wants to use the commute, suggest extending the route or detouring through greenways rather than raising speed.
- Weather and temperature affect intensity and duration planning only; they never override the safety-first principle: cut duration in heat, downgrade or switch to the trainer in rain, fog or ice.
